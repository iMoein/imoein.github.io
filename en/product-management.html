What does a Product Manager do, and what makes the technical role different?

Product management is not simply writing feature requests or maintaining a task backlog. It starts with identifying whose problem we are solving, what constraints matter and how we will recognise a successful outcome. A Product Manager connects user needs, commercial goals and the reality of delivery.

A Technical Product Manager brings that product judgement into conversations about system dependencies, API contracts, data quality, security, reliability and maintenance costs. The aim is not to replace engineers: it is to make better-informed decisions about scope, risk and delivery order.

How I approach the product lifecycle

1. Discover the real problem

Before suggesting a feature, I use data, user conversations and the current workflow to understand the problem. I separate symptoms from causes: slow operations, for instance, do not automatically call for another dashboard.

2. Define outcomes and priorities

I translate needs into observable outcomes, acceptance criteria and explicit risks. A roadmap is a set of priorities and hypotheses to test, not an immutable list of features.

3. Shape an MVP and deliver incrementally

I narrow the first release to a useful solution that can test an assumption. Incremental delivery, feature testing and close engineering collaboration help surface incorrect decisions early.

4. Measure and improve

After launch, I look at actual usage, experience quality, task completion and operational measures. Shipping a feature alone does not demonstrate that it creates value.

Where have I applied this approach?

Channelead — B2B hotel booking platform

Organisational users, first-hand hotel supply, sales workflows and API integrations depend on each other. Product decisions in this setting must account for real availability, booking processes and operational constraints.

Farvah Cafe — café operations software

When a product grows from an MVP, a polished interface is not enough. Order-taking, operational roles, customer interactions and the potential to adapt the software for other cafés need to be considered together.

Gardesh — multimedia platform in development

The platform brings decisions about publisher roles, content workflows, plan limits, media storage and audience experience into the same product. Storage and streaming constraints are practical inputs to the roadmap.

Besoye Samyar Safar — data and reporting

Internal reporting products also have real users. Making metrics understandable and reducing manual work can create as much value as a customer-facing feature.

Which metrics tell me a product is succeeding?

The right measures depend on the product. In booking, successful completion and supply reliability matter; in an operations tool, task time and error rates; in a media service, meaningful audience usage and stable delivery. A few decision-relevant measures are more useful than a dashboard full of numbers.

Alongside outcomes, I track requirement quality, cross-team dependencies, user feedback and service health. Product success is neither just a sales figure nor simply the number of features shipped.
